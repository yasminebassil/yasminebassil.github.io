research

How do we find our way — and what changes as we age?

My doctoral research asked how the aging brain builds and uses maps of the world, using immersive virtual reality, neuroimaging, and brain stimulation. Along the way, I built the tools and infrastructure that made those questions answerable.

The question

Getting lost in a familiar place is one of the earliest and most distressing changes many people notice as they age. But navigation isn't one skill — it's several, working together. You can find your way by remembering a sequence of turns, or by holding a map-like sense of how places relate to each other. These two strategies rely on different brain systems, and they don't age the same way.

My dissertation, Navigating Aging: Characterizing Behavioral and Neural Correlates of Aging-Related Deficits in Human Spatial Navigation, asked which parts of navigation change with age, and what's happening in the brain when they do.

The technical version: I studied age-related differences in reference frame utilization — specifically the formation and recall of allocentric (map-like) versus egocentric (route-based) spatial representations — and the cortical circuits supporting them.

The approach

Studying navigation in a lab is hard: the environments people navigate in real life are large, messy, and full of landmarks, while most lab tasks are small and abstract. So I built a city.

Behavior
Immersive VR
A city-scale virtual environment participants explore freely, paired with standardized clinical and cognitive assessments.
Structure & function
Neuroimaging
fMRI and structural MRI to map the networks engaged during spatial memory and recall.
Causal test
TMS-fMRI
Concurrent brain stimulation and imaging — perturbing a region and watching how the rest of the network responds.

Scale: 110+ older adult participants, recruited through community partnerships and shared across 5 labs.

What we're learning

People do form map-like knowledge of a brand-new city after only a short time exploring it — even when nothing asked them to.

Bassil, Y., Kanukolanu, A., Funderburg, E., Brown, T. I., & Borich, M. R. (2025). Formation of allocentric representations after exposure to a novel, naturalistic, city-like, virtual reality environment. Neuropsychologia. Read →

Aging doesn't erase that map-like knowledge uniformly — older adults fall into distinct profiles of what they recall and how.

Bassil, Y., Kanukolanu, A., Funderburg, E., Cui, E. Z., Brown, T. I., & Borich, M. R. (2026). Distinct aging-related profiles of allocentric knowledge recall following navigation in an immersive, naturalistic, city-like environment. Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience. Read →

Concurrent TMS-fMRI is powerful but notoriously difficult to run well — so an international group of us wrote the field's first consensus guidelines for doing it rigorously.

Woolgar, A., Feredoes, E., Assem, M., Bassil, Y., Bergmann, T. O., Beynel, L., ... & Sack, A. T. (2026). Consensus guidelines for the use of concurrent TMS-fMRI in cognitive and clinical neuroscience. Nature Protocols. Read →

What I built

Answering these questions meant building the infrastructure to ask them. All of it is open-source.

NavCity A city-scale virtual environment and navigation task toolkit, built in Unity. GitHub →
NavCity Analysis The analysis pipeline for NavCity behavioral data. GitHub →
MR.Flow A GUI-based neuroimaging workbench for fMRI preprocessing and analysis. I owned the front-end and UX/UI design. GitLab →
Concurrent TMS-fMRI Established as a shared institutional capability at Emory, opening the method to the broader Atlanta research community.

Also built: lab-wide documentation systems, standard operating procedures, and data pipelines (Python, R, bash/zsh) across 7 GitHub repositories — the unglamorous infrastructure that lets a lab keep working after any one person leaves.
